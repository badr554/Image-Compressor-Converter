WebP vs JPG

WebP vs JPG: Which Format Should You Use?

The short answer

Use WebP when you control a modern website and want a compact image with broad current browser support. Use JPG when compatibility with older software, cameras, forms, or people’s workflows is the priority. Both formats are lossy in their usual photographic use, so both can discard detail when encoded. WebP also supports transparency and a lossless mode; ordinary JPEG does not support transparency. The right choice depends on how the file will be delivered and edited, not on a universal ranking.

For example, a 2400 × 1600 travel photograph exported for a responsive article can be tested as both formats at similar visual quality. A transparent product cutout should not be saved as JPG unless you intentionally replace the transparent areas with a solid background. A photo sent to a legacy office system may be safer as JPG.

How the formats differ

JPEG (commonly named JPG) is a long established photographic format supported by cameras, operating systems, office software, and websites. Its strength is predictable compatibility. Its lossy encoding works well for continuous-tone photos, while fine text, sharp interface edges, and repeated re-saving can expose ringing or block-like artifacts. JPEG does not preserve alpha transparency.

WebP was designed for web delivery and supports lossy and lossless encoding, plus alpha transparency and animation. A WebP file is often smaller than a visually similar JPEG, but the exact difference varies with image content, encoder, quality setting, and dimensions. A flat illustration, foliage photograph, and noisy night scene do not compress in the same way. Treat any percentage as a test result for a particular image, not a guarantee.

Quality and file size

Compare outputs at the same displayed dimensions and judge them at normal viewing scale. A quality slider value is not a standardized measure across encoders, so “80” in one tool is not necessarily equivalent to “80” in another. Look at skin, hair, gradients, small lettering, and high contrast edges. Those regions reveal different failures.

A useful workflow is to create two candidates from the same original, then zoom to 100% only to inspect defects. If WebP is visibly comparable and the receiving site accepts it, use it. If you see banding or smearing, raise quality or choose a different format. Do not repeatedly recompress a JPEG; go back to the original whenever possible because each lossy generation may compound artifacts.

Compatibility and editing

For a website, WebP is a sensible delivery format when your content pipeline, CMS, and target browsers handle it. Keep a source master in a format suitable for future editing. Many teams retain a high quality original and generate derivatives from it instead of treating the web export as the master. For email attachments, shared folders, or unfamiliar systems, JPEG often causes fewer surprises.

A transparent logo is a special case. WebP can preserve its transparent background; JPG cannot. PNG is also a common choice for logos and screenshots with crisp edges, although it can be larger for photographs. Use PNG to WebP when you want to evaluate a transparent or photographic PNG as WebP, and check the transparency in the target app.

A repeatable decision process

Start from an uncompressed or high quality original. Set the target dimensions first; encoding a 4000-pixel-wide image and then displaying it at 800 pixels wastes bytes. Export a WebP candidate and a JPEG candidate. Compare visual quality, byte size, and whether the destination accepts the format. Choose based on all three.

For a profile picture that must upload to an old portal, a JPG may be the right practical answer even if a WebP is smaller. For a modern product page, WebP may be a better transfer format, provided you preserve an original and have a fallback plan in the publishing system. Try PNG to JPG, JPG to PNG, and image compression.

Common mistakes

Do not rename a .webp file to .jpg; renaming changes the label, not the encoded data. Do not assume the extension alone tells the truth if a file came from an unknown source. Do not flatten transparency accidentally by converting a logo to JPEG. Do not use a tiny quality value simply to reach an arbitrary file size without checking the result. If a platform specifies a maximum size, use that as a ceiling and retain enough quality for the real viewing context.

Conclusion

WebP is a flexible web format with useful transparency and often efficient compression; JPG remains a dependable choice for photographs and broad compatibility. Run a small comparison with your actual image and destination. If you want to reduce a JPEG before sharing, start with the 100 KB compressor when that limit is relevant, then inspect the downloaded file.

Frequently asked questions

Which format creates the smallest image?

It depends on image content, dimensions, encoder, and quality. Compare exports from the same original and inspect at the intended size.

Will conversion restore lost detail?

No. A conversion cannot recreate information discarded during an earlier lossy export. Return to the original when available.

Should I delete my original?

Keep it if you may need future edits, a different crop, or another format. Delivery copies are not source masters.
