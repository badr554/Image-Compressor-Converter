There is no single best format

Image formats encode different kinds of information and solve different problems. A camera photograph has continuous tones and texture. A screenshot has crisp edges and text. A logo may need transparent pixels or infinitely scalable vectors. Choosing one format for all three can create unnecessary size or visible defects. Start by identifying what must remain intact and where the result will be opened.

Also distinguish a working master from a delivery file. Keep a high quality or editable source, then export copies for web pages, messaging, print, or upload forms. A delivery format is optimized for a particular use; it does not need to replace the original.

JPEG and JPG

JPEG is a common lossy format for photographs. It is supported by a wide range of devices and applications, which makes it a reliable option for sharing and older upload workflows. JPEG does not support alpha transparency. Repeated saves can introduce additional artifacts, particularly around fine edges and detailed textures, so return to the original when making a new export.

Choose JPEG when interoperability matters or when a destination requests it. Avoid it for assets whose transparent background must remain transparent or for small text that must stay crisp. Compare a PNG to JPG conversion only when a solid background is acceptable.

PNG

PNG uses lossless compression and supports transparency. It is often suitable for interface screenshots, diagrams, simple graphics, and logos with alpha. For photographic images, a PNG may be much larger than a lossy alternative because it preserves the pixel information rather than discarding detail. A PNG can still be optimized or quantized, but color reduction may change gradients and subtle tones.

Use PNG when edge quality or transparency matters and the file size is acceptable. If a photograph is stored as PNG, compare it with WebP or JPEG to see whether a lossy format meets the visual need at a smaller size. Keep the PNG master if its exact pixels are important.

WebP

WebP supports lossy and lossless encoding, transparency, and animation. It is useful for web delivery because one format handles a variety of image types. The resulting size varies with the image and encoder; no fixed savings percentage applies to every file. Verify compatibility within the full publishing pipeline, especially older editing tools or upload forms.

WebP can serve as a compact delivery copy while a separate master remains available for editing. Test transparent edges and fine detail, and compare it with JPEG for photographs. See WebP vs JPG and try PNG to WebP conversion.

SVG, GIF, and newer formats

SVG stores vector shapes and text instructions rather than a fixed photographic pixel grid. It suits many logos, icons, and diagrams when the source is genuine vector artwork. Raster photos embedded in SVG do not become scalable vectors. GIF is known for simple animation and limited-color graphics, but its palette and age make it a poor default for modern photographs.

Newer formats such as AVIF can offer strong compression in supported pipelines, but conversion, editing, and platform acceptance should be checked. “Newest” does not automatically mean best for an existing workflow.

Choose by task

For a photograph emailed to someone using unknown software, JPEG is usually a safe delivery choice. For a transparent app icon, PNG or SVG may fit depending on whether the artwork is raster or vector. For a website image needing transparency and compact delivery, WebP is worth testing. For line art, a real SVG master can remain sharp at multiple sizes. For a screenshot with fine labels, avoid settings that blur text.

If your destination gives a specific format, follow it and verify the upload. Otherwise make a side-by-side comparison at the same dimensions. Check appearance, output bytes, transparency, and editing support. The JPG to PNG converter changes encoding but cannot reverse JPEG quality loss.

Preserve a good source

A reliable archive includes the original capture or editable source, plus derived versions with meaningful filenames. A delivery export should not be the only copy if you expect future crop changes, color adjustments, or format changes. Avoid chains of conversions: each lossy step can accumulate changes, and converting a lossy file into a lossless container does not restore discarded information.

For websites, keep responsive dimensions in mind. Different screen sizes may need different candidates. Optimize the actual files the page serves and inspect the resulting display, not just a design preview. For a practical process, read how to compress images for the web and how to reduce file size while preserving visible quality.

Conclusion

Choose formats by content and destination: JPEG for broad photo compatibility, PNG for lossless pixels and transparency, WebP for flexible web delivery, SVG for vector artwork, and GIF only when its animation constraints suit the task. Keep a source master and compare real exports before committing.

Frequently asked questions

Which format creates the smallest image?

It depends on image content, dimensions, encoder, and quality. Compare exports from the same original and inspect at the intended size.

Will conversion restore lost detail?

No. A conversion cannot recreate information discarded during an earlier lossy export. Return to the original when available.

Should I delete my original?

Keep it if you may need future edits, a different crop, or another format. Delivery copies are not source masters.
